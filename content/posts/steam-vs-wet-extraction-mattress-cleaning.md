Both processes get called "mattress cleaning," which is why the conversation usually goes in circles. They are different technologies with different failure modes, and the difference is measurable.

## The core difference: water as carrier vs. heat as the tool

Hot-water extraction uses water as a carrier. You inject a heated detergent solution into the surface, then vacuum it back out along with what it lifted. On carpet, that works well — the fibers and the pad underneath can take it and survive a slow dry.

A mattress core is foam, latex, or fiber batting. Once it takes on water inside a closed bedroom, it does not dry in a day. The moisture you cannot see is the part that matters, because damp foam is the condition that favors the microbial growth and odor you were trying to get rid of in the first place.

Dry vapor steam carries heat with very little water — around 5% moisture content against the 40–60% typical of extraction. At the nozzle the vapor is superheated, so it lifts and neutralizes what has accumulated in the quilting and seam channels while leaving the core essentially dry.

## Field-by-field

| | Wet extraction | Dry vapor steam |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Moisture into the mattress | High | Very low, roughly 5% |
| Drying time | Often 24–48 hours | Typically 30–60 minutes |
| What it reaches | Surface soil and visible spots | Surface plus upper layers, seams, quilting, edges |
| Heat at the surface | Limited by water temperature | Superheated vapor, calibrated per mattress |
| Chemistry | Detergent and fragrance left in the foam | No chemical residue |
| Verification | Visual result | Pre/post surface reading, ATP on fluid treatments |
| Equipment designed for | Floors and upholstery | Mattresses and sleep surfaces |

## Where extraction genuinely wins

We are not going to pretend extraction has no place. If a mattress has been flooded — a significant fluid volume, a mattress that spent a week soaked, a situation where you need volume flushed out of padding in one pass — an extraction step is genuinely useful, and we say so when we hit it.

What we will not do is run a floor-cleaning process on a sleep surface as the default. The default matters more than the exception, because the default is what happens to every customer who does not know to ask.

## The question that settles it

Ask any provider one thing: *what is the moisture content of your process, and how long until the mattress is dry?*

If the answer is measured in days, they are putting water into foam. That is not a cleaning preference, it is a physics problem — and the fix is not a fan.

## What this means for your mattress type

- **Memory foam.** Dense and slow to release moisture. This is the build least tolerant of a soaked core.
- **Latex.** Resilient and long-lived, but also holds water in ways that are hard to reverse.
- **Hybrid.** Mixed construction — coil support with foam comfort layers. The foam layers are the vulnerable part.
- **Innerspring.** The most forgiving, and still not something to soak inside a closed bedroom.
- **Organic and natural builds.** Often the most sensitive to moisture of all. Our [documented jobs](/gallery) include an Avocado mattress with its foam topper.
