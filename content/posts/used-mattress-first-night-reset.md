A used mattress shows up in a lot of different ways: Grandma's spare room, a marketplace listing, a hand-me-down after a sibling upgraded, the mattress already in the rental you just signed. In every case it has a history nobody wrote down, and you are about to spend eight hours a night on it.

## First, look at it in daylight

Before booking anything, do a two-minute assessment:

- **Read the law tag.** It tells you the construction and often the age. A mattress with a manufacturing date fifteen years back has lived a life.
- **Look at the top surface in raking light** for staining, especially rings or irregular patches that suggest fluid history.
- **Check the seams and piping** — this is where soil and debris collect and where a wet wand never reaches.
- **Press the edges.** A collapsed edge means structural failure, and no cleaning process fixes that.
- **Lift a corner and check the underside.** Wicking ends up on the bottom panel.
- **Smell it after ten minutes out of the room**, not immediately. Noses adapt to a room fast.

If the core is visibly saturated, if the cover is torn through, or if springs are palpable, stop here. Replacement is the right answer.

## Why low moisture matters more on a used mattress

A used mattress has already absorbed years of ambient humidity, and its foam has already been through whatever the previous owner put it through. Adding a wet extraction pass on top of that is a much bigger risk than doing it to a new mattress — especially if the mattress is a memory foam or organic build.

That is the specific case where dry vapor steam is not just a preference. It is the method that does the work without making the existing condition worse.
